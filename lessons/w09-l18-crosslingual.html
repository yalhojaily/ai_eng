Cross-lingual retrieval

Most of the world's technical writing is in a handful of languages, and most of the world's people ask questions in others. A system that lets someone ask in Arabic and be answered from English sources is among the most useful things you can build with these tools, and the hardest test of everything in this phase.

Answer to last lesson's question. BM25 breaks first and completely: an Arabic query shares no tokens with English text, so every score is zero. Dense retrieval with a multilingual model still works, to a degree. The reranker works if it is multilingual. Generation works, since large models read both languages. So the failure is concentrated at the very first stage, which is where this lesson concentrates.

1. The lexical wall

Lesson 13's vocabulary mismatch was a matter of degree: paraphrases share some words. Across scripts the mismatch is total.

Count the shared words

Notice the fourth preset. Bilingual speakers often keep the technical term in English inside an Arabic sentence. Linguists call this code-switching. Here it rescues word matching by accident, which is a reason to keep BM25 in a cross-lingual pipeline.

2. Three ways through the wall

StrategyHowStrengthWeakness
Translate the querya model turns the Arabic query into English, then search as usualall your English machinery works again, BM25 includedone extra model call; a mistranslated query retrieves confidently wrong passages

Multilingual embeddingsembed query and passages with one model that covers both languagesno translation at all; simpledense only; quality varies by language pair; rare terms suffer

These are retrievers with different failure modes, so the lesson of Lesson 15 applies directly: run more than one and fuse the rankings. A common and strong design is multilingual dense search on the original query, plus BM25 and dense search on a translated query, combined by reciprocal rank fusion and then reranked.

3. How one model can embed two languages

Lesson 2's embeddings put texts with similar meanings close together. A multilingual embedding model extends "similar meaning" across languages: a sentence and its translation should land on nearly the same point. Such models are trained on large numbers of translation pairs, with an objective that pulls each pair together and pushes unrelated sentences apart. One well-known recipe starts from a good English model and trains a multilingual student to reproduce the English model's vector both for an English sentence and for its translation.

The result is a shared space, and it is not equally good everywhere. Languages with plenty of parallel text are aligned well. Others are aligned loosely. And specialised vocabulary, the grammatical terms your corpus is full of, is thinly represented in any general training set. This is Lesson 1's language tax appearing in a third currency: first tokens, then cost, now retrieval quality.

4. What goes wrong in translation

Translating a search query is harder than translating a paragraph, because a query is short and has no context to settle its meaning.

  • Terms of art. Arabic grammar has its own precise, centuries-old vocabulary. المثنى is "the dual", not "the double" or "the second". A general translation that misses the technical sense retrieves nothing useful.
  • Ambiguity. One word, several readings: ذهب again, or عين, which may be an eye, a spring of water, or a spy. A paragraph would decide; a two-word query does not.
  • Categories that do not line up. The traditions carve grammar differently. Arabic grammar sorts words into three classes, اسم, فعل and حرف; Universal Dependencies has seventeen tags. A question about حرف maps to several English terms at once.
  • Names. Proper names cross scripts by sound, in several competing spellings, and word matching treats each spelling as a different word.
  • Varieties. A query in a regional dialect, against a model trained mostly on formal written Arabic, is a further step away.

The practical defence is to stop asking for the translation. Ask the model for two or three, covering the technical sense and any other plausible readings, search with all of them, and fuse. This is called query expansion, and it helps in a single language too.

5. Answering across languages

Generation adds its own decisions:

  • Answer in the user's language. State it in the prompt. Left alone, a model facing English passages often drifts into English.
  • Quote in the source language. The citation gate from Lesson 17 checks quotes character by character, so quotes must stay exactly as written. Show the original quote, with a translation beside it clearly marked as a translation.
  • Keep technical terms recoverable. Give the Arabic term with the English one in brackets on first use, so the reader can search the sources themselves.
  • Check faithfulness across languages. The judge now decides whether an English quote entails an Arabic sentence. Validate it on bilingual examples you label yourself; do not assume your English kappa carries over.

6. Measure by language, always

An overall score for a bilingual system is an average of two different systems. Report every metric from Lessons 16 and 17 separately for each query language, and state the gap. That gap is the cost your non-English users are paying, and a number you can drive down: with a better multilingual model, with query translation, with a glossary of terms added to the translation prompt.

A useful experiment is the translation ceiling. Translate your Arabic test queries into English yourself, carefully, and run those. The score you get is roughly the best any query-translation approach could reach. The distance between it and your automatic pipeline tells you how much is lost in translation, as opposed to retrieval.

Checkpoint

Lab: ask in Arabic, retrieve in English

Time: 2 hours. Use the 15 Arabic queries from Lesson 16 and the whole stack so far.

TRANSLATE_SCHEMA = {
    "type": "object",
    "properties": {"translations": {"type": "array", "items": {"type": "string"}}},
    "required": ["translations"], "additionalProperties": False,
}

TRANSLATE_SYSTEM = """You translate search queries from Arabic into English.
The queries will be run against documentation about grammar and the
Universal Dependencies annotation scheme.
... your briefing: use the standard English linguistic term where one
exists; give up to three translations; if a word is ambiguous, cover
the different readings; a short glossary of terms ...
"""

def translate_query(q):
    return call_json(TRANSLATE_SYSTEM, f"<query>{q}</query>",
                     TRANSLATE_SCHEMA, "translations")["translations"]

def strategies(q):
    en = translate_query(q)
    return {
        "direct dense":    dense_search(q, 50),
        "translate+bm25":  rrf([bm25_search(t, 50) for t in en], top=50),
        "translate+dense": rrf([dense_search(t, 50) for t in en], top=50),
        "all fused":       rrf([dense_search(q, 50)]
                               + [bm25_search(t, 50) for t in en]
                               + [dense_search(t, 50) for t in en], top=50),
    }
  1. Compute recall@5, recall@20 and MRR for the four strategies on the Arabic queries, with and without the reranker. One table.
  2. Put the best Arabic row next to your English results from Lesson 16. How large is the gap?
  3. Translation ceiling. Translate the 15 queries into English by hand and run them through plain hybrid search. How much of the gap is translation, how much retrieval?
  4. Read the model's translations for every query that failed. Label each failure: term of art, ambiguity, category mismatch, or retrieval after a correct translation.
  5. Add six entries to the glossary in TRANSLATE_SYSTEM, drawn from those failures. Rerun and read the flips.
  6. Extend Lesson 17's answer so that the answer is in Arabic while the quotes stay in English. Does the citation gate still pass? Label ten Arabic sentence and English quote pairs for faithfulness and measure your judge's kappa on them.

Reading duties

Phase 4 project: ship the cross-lingual search

Assemble Lessons 13 to 18 into one system: ask a question about grammar in Arabic or English, and get an answer in the same language, grounded in an English corpus, with checkable citations.

What to build

  1. An indexing script: load, chunk with heading paths, embed, store, with the settings recorded.
  2. A search function: hybrid retrieval with normalisation, query translation and expansion for Arabic, fusion, reranking.
  3. An answer function: structured output with citations and quotes, the citation gate, and abstention.
  4. A command-line interface: a question in, an answer with its sources out.
  5. The eval, in your harness: retrieval metrics per language, citation-gate pass rate, judged faithfulness, abstention on unanswerable questions.

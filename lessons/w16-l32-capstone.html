2. Six ideas that ran through all of it

  1. A model sees tokens and predicts the next one. Cost, context limits, sampling, the language tax, fine-tuning loss: all follow from that one fact (Lessons 1, 3, 21, 30, 31).
  2. The model knows only what is in its context. Prompts, examples, retrieved passages, tool results and notes are all ways of deciding what goes there (Lessons 5, 7, 13, 20, 21).
  3. Form can be checked by code; truth needs evidence. Schemas, invariants and citation gates catch malformed output. Whether it is right takes gold data or a validated judge (Lessons 4, 8, 11, 17, 23).
  4. Nothing is known until it is measured, and one run is not a measurement. Baselines, confidence intervals, per-item diffs, repeated runs (Lessons 9, 10, 12, 24).
  5. Text that looks like an instruction is still only text. Use and mention, speech acts, injection, tool results as data (Lessons 5, 17, 22, 28).
  6. Language is structured, variable and always moving. Morphology, ambiguity, register, drift. Knowing how language works told you where the systems would fail before they did (every lesson).

Two rules. Integration is the work. The parts exist; what is new is one configuration, one trace across components, one eval command, one place where budgets are enforced. Scope by subtraction. If time is short, drop a capability entirely and say so. A workbench with four capabilities and their numbers beats one with seven and no numbers.

5. The write-up

Four pages at most. A reader who has not seen the code should finish it knowing what the system does, how well, and where not to trust it.

  1. What it is, in a paragraph, with one worked example in each language.
  2. Architecture: one diagram, and for each component the reason it is a single call, a workflow or an agent (Lesson 19).
  3. Results: one table with a row per capability and its metrics, each beside a baseline. State the size of every test set (Lesson 9).
  4. The Arabic–English gap: for every metric you have in both languages. What narrowed it, and what did not.
  5. Cost and speed: per request, p50 and p95, and the hosted-or-local decision for the diacritiser (Lesson 31).
  6. Failures: your taxonomy from open coding (Lesson 25), with counts, and three examples you could not fix.
  7. What you would not let it do unsupervised, and why.
  8. What you would do with another month.

Sections 6 and 7 are the ones that show whether you have become an engineer of these systems. Anyone can report a success. Knowing precisely where your system fails, and saying so, is the skill.

6. Afterwards

The tools in this course will be replaced. Model names, libraries and prices already differ from when these lessons were written. What should last is the method, and it is short enough to carry around:

  • Start with the simplest thing that could work, and climb only when a measurement says to.
  • Build the eval before the second version.
  • Read your system's outputs and traces every week, yourself.
  • Report every result with its baseline, its test-set size and its cost.
  • When a new technique appears, ask which of the six ideas it is an instance of, and what it would do to your numbers.

And keep the linguistics. You chose to learn this field through language, and it paid off in every phase: morphology explained tokenisation, distribution explained embeddings, annotation practice explained evaluation, pragmatics explained injection, register explained drift. The people who build these systems best tend to be the ones most curious about what the systems are modelling.

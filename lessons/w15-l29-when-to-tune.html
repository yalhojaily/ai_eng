When fine-tuning beats prompting and retrieval

For six phases you have changed everything around the model and never the model. That was the right order. This last phase asks when the remaining move, changing the model's own parameters, is worth its cost, and works through one task where it is: restoring the vowel marks of Arabic.

Answer to last lesson's question. Changing the model is the right move when five things hold together. The task is narrow and will not change next month. You can get many examples of correct output. You have an eval, and prompting has stopped improving on it. The failures are failures of skill, not of missing facts. And volume is high enough that a small specialised model would save real money or time. Diacritisation meets all five, which is why it is this phase's task.

1. The ladder, in order

Each step up costs more to build and far more to maintain, so you climb only when the step below has stopped paying, and you know it has because your eval says so (Phase 3). Teams that begin at the top spend a month training a model to do what a paragraph of instructions would have done.

Fine-tuning means continuing a model's training on your own examples, so that its parameters shift toward producing your kind of output. Everything below it on the ladder works through the context window and vanishes when the request ends. Fine-tuning is the only step that makes a lasting change to the model.

2. Is the gap knowledge or skill?

This distinction settles most "should we fine-tune?" debates.

  • A knowledge gap: the model does not have a fact. What your guidelines say, what changed last week, what is in your corpus. The remedy is to supply the fact: retrieval (Phase 4). Fine-tuning is a poor way to store facts. They are absorbed unreliably, cannot be cited, and go stale.
  • A skill gap: the model has the information and cannot do the thing dependably. Follow a format every time, write in a house style, apply a subtle convention across thousands of cases, perform a narrow transformation at low cost. The remedy is practice: examples, and when a handful in the prompt are not enough, many examples in training.

The two combine well. A fine-tuned model can be the reader in a retrieval system, and a small fine-tuned model can be the cheap first stage of a cascade (Lesson 27).

3. This phase's task: tashkeel

Arabic script writes consonants and long vowels. The short vowels, and several other features of pronunciation, are written as small marks above and below the letters, called tashkeel or diacritics, and in ordinary text they are almost always left out. Readers supply them from knowledge of the language. You met the consequence in Lesson 2: ذهب is gold or he went until the marks, or the context, decide.

Restoring the marks is called diacritisation. It matters for text-to-speech, for learners and children's books, for religious and classical texts, and for disambiguation.

One skeleton, several words

Press the four presets in turn. The letters never change, only the marks, and you get a verb, a noun, a passive and a causative. A system that restores marks is choosing among words.

The marks carry two different kinds of information, and this is what makes the task linguistically interesting:

  • Inside the word, the marks say which word it is and in what form: كَتَبَ against كُتُب. Getting these right needs the vocabulary and morphology of Phase 1, and enough context to pick the sense.
  • On the last letter, the mark usually shows the word's case or mood: its grammatical role in the sentence. The subject takes one ending, the object another, a noun after a preposition a third. Arabic grammar calls this system الإعراب. Getting these right needs the syntax of the whole sentence.

So a diacritiser has to be a lexicon and a parser at once. Case endings are consistently the harder half, for people as well as machines, and evaluations of this task report results with and without them for that reason.

4. Measuring diacritisation

Two standard metrics, both simple enough to compute by hand:

  • Diacritic error rate (DER): of all the letters, what share were given the wrong marks?
  • Word error rate (WER): of all the words, what share contain at least one wrongly marked letter?

Each is commonly reported twice: counting every letter, and ignoring the last letter of each word, which removes case endings from the count. The difference between the two versions tells you how much of the error is syntactic.

5. Does this task call for fine-tuning?

ConditionDiacritisation
Narrow and stableOne transformation, and the rules of the language change over generations, not releases.
Plentiful examplesAny vowelled text yields a training pair for free: strip the marks to make the input, and the original is the answer.
An eval existsDER and WER against held-out vowelled text, computed by code.
A skill, not a factThe model must apply morphology and syntax, letter by letter, without drifting. Retrieval cannot do that for it.
Volume and costThe output is longer than the input, since every letter gains a mark, so large models are costly per page. A small model that does only this could run locally.

Two cautions before any training. A large general model may already do the task well enough for your purpose, in which case stop. And the costs of fine-tuning arrive later: when the base model is superseded you retrain, and a tuned model is one more thing to evaluate, host and monitor. So the first job is to measure the prompted baseline. That number is the bar.

6. What do models learn about language?

A fair question at the end of a course on language models: do they contain anything a linguist would recognise? Researchers test this with probing. Take the internal vectors a model produces for each word, and train a very small classifier to predict a linguistic property from them, such as part of speech or syntactic role. If a tiny classifier succeeds, the information must already be present in the vectors.

The findings are suggestive. Parts of speech can be read off early layers, syntactic relations from middle layers, and meaning-related properties from later ones: roughly the order of the classic pipeline in Lesson 8, discovered by a system that was only ever asked to predict the next token. Nobody taught it grammar. Grammar turned out to be useful for prediction.

That is why fine-tuning works with so little data. You are not teaching Arabic morphology and syntax from nothing. You are teaching a model that already encodes a great deal of both to express that knowledge in one specific form: a mark on every letter.

Checkpoint

Lab: data, metrics and the baseline to beat

Time: 2 hours. You need vowelled Arabic text. A standard source is the cleaned Tashkeela benchmark distributed with Fadel et al.'s paper (see the reading; its repository has training, validation and test files). Any fully vowelled text you have will also do.

import json, random, re

MARKS = re.compile(r"[ً-ْ]")          # the eight basic diacritics
strip = lambda s: MARKS.sub("", s)

def units(word):
    """Split a word into (letter, marks) pairs."""
    out = []
    for ch in word:
        if MARKS.match(ch) and out:
            out[-1][1] += ch
        else:
            out.append([ch, ""])
    return [(b, "".join(sorted(m))) for b, m in out]

def score(reference, hypothesis):
    """Returns counts for DER and WER, with and without the last letter of each word."""
    c = {"letters": 0, "wrong": 0, "letters_nc": 0, "wrong_nc": 0, "words": 0, "wrong_words": 0}
    for rw, hw in zip(reference.split(), hypothesis.split()):
        ru, hu = units(rw), units(hw)
        if [b for b, _ in ru] != [b for b, _ in hu]:
            c["words"] += 1; c["wrong_words"] += 1          # the system changed the letters
            c["letters"] += len(ru); c["wrong"] += len(ru)
            c["letters_nc"] += len(ru) - 1; c["wrong_nc"] += len(ru) - 1
            continue
        errors = [r[1] != h[1] for r, h in zip(ru, hu) if "ء" <= r[0] <= "ي"]
        if not errors:
            continue                                          # punctuation or a number: nothing to mark
        c["letters"] += len(errors); c["wrong"] += sum(errors)
        c["letters_nc"] += len(errors) - 1; c["wrong_nc"] += sum(errors[:-1])
        c["words"] += 1; c["wrong_words"] += any(errors)
    return c

lines = [l.strip() for l in open("tashkeela/train.txt", encoding="utf-8") if 20 < len(l) < 300]
random.Random(0).shuffle(lines)

def write(path, rows):
    with open(path, "w", encoding="utf-8") as f:
        for line in rows:
            f.write(json.dumps({"messages": [
                {"role": "user", "content": "أضف التشكيل الكامل إلى النص التالي دون تغيير أي حرف:\n" + strip(line)},
                {"role": "assistant", "content": line}]}, ensure_ascii=False) + "\n")

write("data/train.jsonl", lines[:4000])
write("data/valid.jsonl", lines[4000:4300])
write("data/test.jsonl",  lines[4300:4500])

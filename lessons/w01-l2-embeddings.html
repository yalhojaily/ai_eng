Embeddings: meaning as geometry

Lesson 1 ended with a puzzle: token IDs are arbitrary numbers, so where does a model keep the fact that cat and kitten are related? The answer is an old idea from linguistics, turned into arithmetic.

1. The first thing a model does with a token

A token ID is a row number. The model owns a big table, the embedding table, with one row per vocabulary entry. Each row is a list of numbers, a vector, typically hundreds or thousands long. The very first step of the model is a lookup: replace every ID with its row.

The numbers shown are invented. The real ones are not written by anyone: they start random and are adjusted during training, along with every other parameter, to make next-token prediction better. So the answer to the puzzle is this: the IDs carry no meaning, the rows do. Training pushes the rows for cat and kitten close together, because treating them alike helps prediction.

Why would it help? Because of how language works.

2. The linguist's idea: meaning is the company a word keeps

In the 1950s the linguists Zellig Harris and J. R. Firth argued that words occurring in similar contexts tend to have similar meanings. Firth's version is the famous one: You shall know a word by the company it keeps. This is the distributional hypothesis.

Test it on yourself. You have never seen the word wug, but read: "She fed the wug. The wug slept on the sofa. The wug chased a mouse." You now know roughly what a wug is, purely from its neighbours.

That gives a recipe a computer can follow without understanding anything: for each word, count the words that appear near it. The list of counts is a vector. Words with similar count-lists should be similar in meaning.

3. Measuring "similar": cosine similarity

The neighbour list above needed a number for "how alike are two vectors". The standard choice is cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between them.

  • 1: pointing the same way. Same kind of company.
  • 0: at right angles. Nothing in common.
  • −1: pointing opposite ways.

It is computed from the dot product (multiply matching entries, add them up) divided by the two lengths:

cosine(a, b) = (a · b) / (|a| × |b|)

Dividing by the lengths is the point. A frequent word has big counts and so a long vector, but length says how often a word appears, not what it means. Angle ignores length.

Angle matters, length does not

Notice: stretch b and the dot product changes but the cosine does not. Sweep the angle through 90° and the cosine crosses zero.

4. From count-lists to dense embeddings

Count vectors work, but they are awkward: one entry per vocabulary word, so tens of thousands of entries, nearly all zero. These are called sparse vectors.

Modern embeddings are dense: a few hundred to a few thousand numbers, none of them tied to a particular context word, all of them learned. The breakthrough that made this practical was word2vec (Mikolov and colleagues, 2013): instead of counting neighbours, train a small network to predict a word's neighbours, then keep the learned row for each word. Predicting company and counting company capture the same information; prediction just compresses it.

A language model's embedding table is the same idea, learned as a side effect of next-token prediction. The famous party trick. In good word embeddings, directions carry meaning: the step from man to woman is close to the step from king to queen, so king − man + woman lands near queen. It is real, and you will run it in the lab, but it works for some relations much better than others. Treat it as evidence that the geometry encodes structure, not as a reliable tool.

5. One row per word is not enough

Everything so far gives each word a single vector. Linguists will spot the problem at once: words have several senses. Decide the sense in each sentence below.

You resolved every one using the surrounding words. A table lookup cannot: it returns the same row for bank every time, a blur of all its senses. Arabic makes this sharper, because short vowels are normally left unwritten, so ذهب is both the noun gold and the verb he went until context decides. Vectors of this kind are called static embeddings.

This is what the rest of a language model is for. The layers after the lookup repeatedly mix each token's vector with the vectors around it, so that what comes out for bank in a sentence about rivers differs from what comes out in a sentence about loans. Those are contextual embeddings.

One more step gets you to the tool you will actually use. An embedding model reads a whole sentence or passage and returns a single vector for all of it. Two passages that mean similar things get nearby vectors, even with no words in common, and with a multilingual model, even across languages.

6. Why an engineer cares

"Turn text into a vector, compare vectors" is one of the two or three most-used operations in applied AI:

  • Semantic search. Embed every document once, embed the query, return the nearest. This is the core of retrieval in Phase 4.
  • Clustering and deduplication. Group support tickets, find near-identical passages.
  • Classification with few examples. Label a text by its nearest labelled neighbours.
  • Evaluation. A rough check of whether an answer means the same as a reference.

Three practical facts to carry forward. Vectors from different embedding models are not comparable, so if you change model you re-embed everything. Similarity is not truth: "the drug is safe" and "the drug is not safe" share almost all their company and usually land close together. And quality varies by language, which you should measure, not assume.

Checkpoint

Lab: real embeddings

Time: 45 minutes. You need: pip install gensim numpy. The first run downloads a set of pretrained English word vectors (GloVe, roughly 65 MB).

Part A. Static word vectors. Before running, predict: what are the five nearest neighbours of bank? Will they be about money, rivers, or both?

import numpy as np
import gensim.downloader as api

wv = api.load("glove-wiki-gigaword-50")   # 50 numbers per word

def cosine(a, b):
    return float(np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)))

print(wv["cat"][:8])                       # a row of the table
print(cosine(wv["cat"], wv["kitten"]))     # your function
print(wv.similarity("cat", "kitten"))      # the library's: should match
print(cosine(wv["cat"], wv["coffee"]))

print(wv.most_similar("bank", topn=5))
print(wv.most_similar(positive=["king", "woman"], negative=["man"], topn=3))
  1. Check your cosine against wv.similarity.
  2. Compare the neighbours of bank with your prediction. What does the result tell you about static embeddings?
  3. Try five analogies of your own in different areas: capital cities, verb tenses (walking : walked :: swimming : ?), plurals, opposites. Record which relations work and which fail.

Part B. Sentence vectors across languages (optional, larger download). pip install sentence-transformers, then:

from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

model = SentenceTransformer("paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2")
sentences = [
    "The students will write their lessons in the library.",
    "سيكتب الطلاب دروسهم في المكتبة.",
    "He deposited the money in the bank.",
    "We sat on the bank of the river.",
    "The drug is safe.",
    "The drug is not safe.",
]
emb = model.encode(sentences)
print(util.cos_sim(emb, emb).numpy().round(2))
  1. Is the Arabic sentence closest to its English translation? That is cross-lingual similarity with no shared words at all.
  2. How similar are the two bank sentences, compared with what Part A led you to expect?
  3. How similar are the two drug sentences? Write one line on what that means for a search system in medicine or law.
What to look for in step 2

A static vector for bank is trained on every occurrence of the string, so its neighbours reflect whichever sense dominates the training text, with the others mixed in or drowned out. Whatever mix you observe, the point is that there is exactly one list: the table cannot give you a "river" list and a "money" list.
